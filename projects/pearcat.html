Animatronic Oz Prize — "most complex/most intricate mechanisms or robots"

PearCat is a competition robot I designed and built for the annual competition in MIT's 2.007: Design and Manufacturing I. It scores by spinning gear-driven platforms at two different heights and by hooking and pulling a swiveling point multiplier, using four main mechanisms: a belt-driven lift, a two-motor gear spinner, an all-wheel drivetrain, and an actuated tow hook. PearCat won the class's Animatronic Oz Prize for the most complex and intricate mechanisms.

Context/Motivation

2.007 is built around an annual competition. Each year, the course staff design a themed gameboard full of objectives and obstacles, and every student designs and fabricates a robot to score as many points as possible within a two-minute round and a strict set of build constraints. My year's gameboard was themed after the recently released movie Wicked.

Problem Framing

Of course I wanted to score plenty of points, but I also wanted to have fun building my robot. That led me to the gear-driven train platform and the hot air balloon — a chance to explore gears and transmissions — and a strategy analysis added the Oz Head point multiplier. The balloon and train gears sat at different heights and spun in opposite directions, so the spinner needed vertical travel and torque in both directions. Completing Train to Oz during the 30-second autonomous period would double its points, which demanded speed and a hands-free start. On the balloon, I targeted 5.1 lb, the lightest weight in its 1.3× tier, since anything heavier would add torque without adding points.

Design Process

Establishing the most critical module

I identified the height-varying gear spinner as my most critical module (MCM). To raise it, I chose a timing-belt lift over lead screws (the ones available seemed too slow), scissor lifts (high torque at the start of travel), and four-bar linkages (overkill for the travel distance). I first planned slots and pins to constrain the carriage, but worried they wouldn't be stiff enough against pitch reaction forces. At a lab instructor's suggestion (thanks Kait!), I made dovetail rails instead, milling them from Delrin for its low friction with guidance from shop staff (thanks Scott!). I sized the first gearbox — a 2:1 ratio driven by a Bilda torque servo — from force-meter measurements and motor curves.

Chassis redesign

My MVP mounted the MCM on a simple box chassis. Once assembled, though, the MCM was unstable in motion, and the chassis underused the volume within the bounding box — bad news for the modules I still had to add (and it could stand to look a bit cooler). I redesigned it with three goals: reduce compliance at the MCM, remove redundant aluminum, and leave room for future modules to integrate cleanly while their final forms were still uncertain. I had the new parts waterjet-cut to minimize misalignment and add stability (thanks Bill!).

Diagnosing the gearbox shortfall

My first full Train to Oz trial failed: the single-motor 2:1 gearbox stalled at the easiest train gear. A quickly mocked-up 3:1 version worked, but took 50–60 s at the lowest-torque train gear — far too slow for the autonomous period, and enough to eat into the time I needed for other objectives. I traced the failure back to three oversights in my original sizing:

  • I measured the force Train to Oz required with no load, though the robot itself would be a non-negligible load.
  • I assumed a higher voltage than the stock batteries provided; a lower voltage meant a lower torque-speed curve, so my gear ratio was undersized.
  • I assumed the motors would spin fast enough, never checking the speed needed to finish the obstacle in under 30 s.

I carefully rederived the torque requirement — as well as my speed requirement — realizing I was a ways away from my goal. However, I had to make the hard choice to prioritize actually bringing the rest of my modules into existence to finish my robot, rather than invest time into something that already existed (albeit not very well).

Speed-running the drivetrain and tow hook

With about three weeks before impound and several modules unbuilt, I kept these modules simple and fast to fabricate, saving time to iterate on the gearbox. 3D-printed wheel caps doubled as spacers and fasteners. Manually adjustable A-shaped aluminum slides tensioned the drive belts, avoiding cutting and rejoining belts, more complex spring tensioners, or re-cutting the chassis.

The tow hook, driven by a 217:1 LDO motor (the highest-torque option) to pull a measured 15 N on the Oz Head, stows to fit the starting box and hooks upward — loading the back wheels for traction, following the pushback tractor principle. I opted for the simplest execution of the hook, using an L-shaped aluminum piece bent along its length and a laser-cut acrylic motor adapter; when the acrylic adapter broke, I replaced it with a steel adapter.

Dual-motor gearbox

To fix the spinner, I increased power in two ways. First, I fed the gearbox directly from the LiPo battery, raising its torque-speed curve. Then I moved to two Bilda Speed motors driving a 3:1 gearbox — about 3.18 N·m and 25 RPM at peak power. Space was the main constraint, since the bounding box had been sized for one motor: I shifted the timing-belt clamps inward and extended their shafts to double as spacers, and while the belts ran slightly crooked, the spacers kept them from slipping. When torque at the claw later made set screws slip, I filed the output axle into a D-shaft and made a custom claw adaptor, finalizing the gearbox.

Autonomous start

Each round opened with a 30-second autonomous period, in which the robot had to start and run with hands off the manual controls. To trigger it, I built a simple photoresistor circuit feeding one of the Arduino's analog pins, and coded a routine to complete the train obstacle and reach Oz. When I flashed a light at the robot, the Arduino detected the spike in light intensity and launched the routine. In competition, the autonomous period ran flawlessly.

Room for Improvement

In competition, PearCat ran its autonomous period flawlessly — then couldn't move its belt drive or tow hook. Different parts of the robot ran on separate batteries, a 4.8 V four-pack and a LiPo, and I, nervous on competition day, had only switched on the LiPo. PearCat scored decently but didn't advance. An earlier problem in the build had a similar root cause: under pressure to move quickly, I sized the first gearbox from incomplete assumptions and redesigned the chassis before testing it, spending time on a module that wasn't my riskiest.

Outcome

  • Animatronic Oz Prize for most complex/intricate mechanisms
  • Flash-triggered autonomous start; autonomous period ran flawlessly in competition
  • Train to Oz time: 50–60 s (single motor) → 19–24 s (dual motor) — 2× faster at worst, over 3× at best
  • Spinner output: ~3.18 N·m, 25 RPM at peak power

Reflection

Redesigning the chassis before testing the gearbox taught me the value of prioritizing the hard way: now I build and test the riskiest module against its real requirements before building around it. Competition day added a smaller, humbler lesson — a machine is only as reliable as the checklist you run before it starts.
